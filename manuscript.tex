\begin{abstract}
Text metadata is the most readily available description of public chromatin
accessibility data, but it is not known how well it reflects similarity
between the underlying chromatin profiles. We compared pairwise cosine
similarity matrices from 12 embedding types across 32,245 human assay for
transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) experiments from
ChIP-Atlas. The embeddings were two chromatin representations (Atacformer and
regulatory potential, RP), three biomedical language models and one
general-domain model applied to full metadata and to cell-type names alone,
term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF), and a random baseline. We
measured agreement with Spearman rank correlation, Mantel permutation tests,
and top-$k$ neighborhood overlap. On the full dataset, text embeddings
correlated with Atacformer similarity at $\rho = 0.20$ to $0.30$. This
agreement was driven mostly by experiments with few called peaks, which
consist largely of promoter peaks (median 69 to 75\% within 1~kb of a
transcription start site for experiments with 100 to 10,000 peaks) and
therefore look alike in both chromatin representations regardless of cell
type. Among the 17,232 experiments with at least 5,000 peaks, the correlation
with Atacformer fell to $\rho = 0.011$ to $0.016$, with less than 0.1\% of
top-5 neighbors shared. Agreement with RP was weak but persisted
($\rho = 0.06$ to $0.10$; 28 to 35\% of top-5 neighbors shared), consistent
with both capturing coarse cell-type identity. Most of the full-dataset text
signal came from sample titles, which mainly identify the submission and
sequencing platform. TF-IDF matched or exceeded the language models in every
correlation comparison. Text metadata is at best a coarse proxy for chromatin
similarity, and peak count should be controlled whenever chromatin
representations are compared across public repositories.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid growth of epigenomics databases such as ChIP-Atlas~\cite{chipatlas},
Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE)~\cite{encode}, and Roadmap
Epigenomics~\cite{roadmap} has produced tens of thousands of ATAC-seq
experiments across diverse human cell types and conditions. Organizing,
retrieving, and comparing these experiments requires similarity measures that
reflect the underlying biology. A natural question is how far the text
metadata attached to each experiment (cell-type labels and descriptions, and
sample titles) can serve as a proxy for chromatin accessibility similarity.

This question has direct practical implications. If text embeddings of
experimental metadata closely approximate chromatin-based similarity measures
(such as those derived from Atacformer or RP), they could enable
natural-language retrieval and annotation of ATAC-seq data without access to
the raw signal, which would make large epigenomics repositories easier to use.
If agreement is limited, the results instead point to fundamental limits on
what text can capture and motivate investment in chromatin-specific
representation learning.

Recent advances in biomedical natural language processing have produced
powerful text encoders pretrained on large biomedical corpora.
PubMedBERT~\cite{pubmedbert} is pretrained from scratch on PubMed abstracts and
full-text articles. SapBERT~\cite{sapbert} and MedCPT~\cite{medcpt} are both
initialized from a pretrained PubMedBERT checkpoint and further fine-tuned for
different objectives: SapBERT through self-alignment on Unified Medical
Language System (UMLS) synonym pairs, and MedCPT through contrastive learning on
PubMed search logs. These models perform well on biomedical entity
recognition, relation extraction, and information retrieval, yet whether their
representations capture the biological relationships encoded in chromatin
accessibility data has not been systematically evaluated.

Atacformer~\cite{atacformer} is a transformer trained on ATAC-seq data with a
triplet margin loss so that its embeddings cluster by cell type and chromatin
state. Regulatory potential (RP)~\cite{lisa2} is a simpler, gene-centered
alternative in which each gene is scored by the peaks near its transcription
start site (TSS), weighted by their distance from it.

We compare pairwise cosine similarity matrices from 12 embedding types (two
chromatin representations, four language models applied to full metadata and
to cell-type names, TF-IDF, and a random baseline) across 32,245 curated human
ATAC-seq experiments, treating both agreement and disagreement as informative
findings. Public repositories mix experiments of very different quality, so we
also examine how the number of called peaks affects these comparisons, and we
base our primary analysis on experiments with at least 5,000 peaks.

The key findings are:
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
  \setlength{\topsep}{0pt}
  \item Peak count strongly shapes similarity in both chromatin
        representations. Experiments with few peaks consist mostly of promoter
        peaks, and they inflate apparent text-chromatin agreement on the full
        dataset.
  \item In experiments with at least 5,000 peaks, text barely tracks
        Atacformer similarity and tracks RP only weakly, mostly through coarse
        cell-type identity.
  \item TF-IDF matches all four language models. On the full dataset, most of
        the text signal comes from sample titles, which reflect data provenance
        more than biology.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset}

We obtained 48,822 human ATAC-seq experiments from ChIP-Atlas (hg38
assembly)~\cite{chipatlas}. We removed 16,567 experiments with corrupted or
empty BED files and 10 experiments producing zero regulatory potential signal
near gene TSSs, leaving a curated set of 32,245 experiments spanning 1,108 cell
types.

We used the ChIP-Atlas peak calls at $q < 10^{-10}$. The number of peaks per
experiment ranges from 1 to 181,134 (median 6,287; interquartile range 780 to
19,836), and 8,964 experiments have fewer than 1,000 peaks. Because
experiments with few peaks retain mostly promoter peaks
(Section~\ref{sec:coverage}), our primary analysis uses the 17,232 experiments
with at least 5,000 peaks, which we call \textit{well-covered} experiments. We
use peak count as a proxy for sequencing depth and library quality, which were
not part of the metadata we used. Results on all 32,245 experiments (the
\textit{full dataset}) are reported for comparison.

\subsection{Atacformer Embeddings}
\label{sec:atacformer}

We encoded each experiment using \url{databio/atacformer-base-hg38}
(AtacformerForCellClustering)~\cite{atacformer}, a transformer trained with a
triplet margin loss. Peak BED files were tokenized using the gtars Tokenizer
with a vocabulary of 890K genomic regions. Up to 1,024 randomly sampled peak
tokens were mean-pooled to produce a 192-dimensional embedding per experiment.

\subsection{Regulatory Potential Embeddings}

We computed RP for each experiment using a LISA2-style exponential decay
model~\cite{lisa2}. Each peak's contribution to a gene is weighted by
$w(d) = e^{-d/10\,\text{kb}}$, where $d$ is the distance to the gene's TSS,
and peaks overlapping annotated exons receive a fixed weight of 1.0 instead.
Summing over all peaks gives a per-gene RP score, and stacking these scores
over all hg38 autosomal gene TSSs maps each experiment to a
40,772-dimensional vector.

\subsection{Text Embeddings}
\label{sec:text_emb}

For each experiment we built a metadata string by concatenating the available
fields: \{cell\_type\}. \{cell\_type\_class\}. \{cell\_type\_description\}.
\{title\}. The description is the ChIP-Atlas cell-type description (for
example, primary tissue and diagnosis), and the title is the experiment title
from the sequencing archive record. We embedded this string with four
encoders, each producing 768-dimensional embeddings. Three are biomedical
models: PubMedBERT~\cite{pubmedbert}
(\url{microsoft/BiomedNLP-BiomedBERT-base-uncased-abstract-fulltext}, mean
pooling), SapBERT~\cite{sapbert}
(\url{cambridgeltl/SapBERT-from-PubMedBERT-fulltext}, classification token
(CLS) pooling), and MedCPT~\cite{medcpt} (\url{ncbi/MedCPT-Query-Encoder},
mean pooling). The fourth is a general-domain sentence
encoder~\cite{mpnet} (\url{sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2}, mean
pooling), which we call General. We refer to the four encoders as language
models (LMs). To isolate the contribution of cell-type identity, we also
encoded the cell-type name alone with all four models (name-only variants). We
additionally computed TF-IDF embeddings (top 2,048 features, sublinear term
frequency, 2,048-dimensional) and a random Gaussian baseline (192-dimensional,
seed 42).

\subsection{Similarity Matrices and Comparison Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

For each embedding type we built pairwise cosine similarity matrices at two
levels.

\textit{Cell-type level.} Experiment embeddings were mean-pooled within each
cell type to give one vector per cell type. A corpus mean vector, computed from
an arbitrary subset of 10,000 experiment embeddings, was subtracted from each
cell-type vector. For every pair of cell types $(i, j)$, the cosine similarity
between their centered vectors was stored in entry $(i, j)$, giving a
symmetric matrix ($1{,}108 \times 1{,}108$ for the full dataset) with ones on
the diagonal.

\textit{Experiment level.} The same centering was applied to individual
experiment embeddings, and the cosine similarity between every pair of
experiments $(i, j)$ was stored in entry $(i, j)$
($32{,}245 \times 32{,}245$ for the full dataset).

Corpus centering corrects for anisotropy in transformer mean-pooled
representations. Without it, embeddings tend to occupy a narrow cone, which
inflates all cosine similarities and hides relative differences between
pairs. The centering vector was estimated from individual experiments rather
than from cell-type means, so that it does not depend on the level of
aggregation.

We compared each text-embedding similarity matrix with each chromatin
reference (Atacformer and RP) in three ways. The main measure was the
Spearman rank correlation ($\rho$) between upper-triangle entries, with
bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) from 1,000 resamples of cell types
or experiments (50,000 randomly sampled pairs per resample). At the cell-type
level we also ran one-sided Mantel permutation tests (1,000 permutations).
Finally, we measured top-$k$ neighborhood overlap, the mean fraction of shared
top-$k$ nearest neighbors, for $k \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$.

For the primary analysis, cell-type vectors were recomputed from well-covered
experiments only (954 cell types), and experiment-level matrices were
restricted to the 17,232 well-covered experiments. The corpus mean used for
centering was unchanged, and experiment-level correlations were computed on
$2 \times 10^6$ randomly sampled pairs.

\subsection{Technical Covariates and Sensitivity Analyses}
\label{sec:tech_methods}

For each experiment we recorded the number of peaks in its BED file and the
fraction of peaks whose midpoint lies within 1~kb of an annotated RefSeq TSS
(the hg38 annotation used for RP). For a pair of experiments with $n_i$ and
$n_j$ peaks, we defined \textit{depth match} as
$-|\log_{10} n_i - \log_{10} n_j|$ and \textit{mean depth} as
$(\log_{10} n_i + \log_{10} n_j)/2$. On the full dataset we used these
quantities in four analyses: (i) Spearman correlations within peak-count
strata, using pairs in which both experiments have fewer than 1,000, 1,000 to
5,000, 5,000 to 10,000, or at least 10,000 peaks; (ii) partial Spearman
correlations controlling for depth match and mean depth, and separately for
indicators of shared cell type and shared cell-type class, computed by
regressing the covariate ranks out of each variable's ranks; (iii) a
title-only TF-IDF embedding with the same settings, fit on the title field
alone and corpus-centered, to isolate the contribution of sample titles; and
(iv) recomputation of the Atacformer, RP, full-text LM, and TF-IDF
similarities without corpus centering. Uncertainty for partial correlations
was estimated with an experiment-level block bootstrap (1,000 resamples of
experiments, 200,000 pairs each).

\subsection{Comparing Language Models with TF-IDF}

To test whether the LMs capture information related to chromatin similarity
beyond vocabulary overlap, we compared the full-text LM embeddings with TF-IDF
in three ways.

\textit{Pairwise similarity scatter.} On the full dataset, we sampled 25,000
random experiment pairs, plotted TF-IDF similarity against LM similarity, and
colored each point by Atacformer similarity. Points above the diagonal are
pairs that the LM scores higher than TF-IDF. We also computed the Spearman
correlation between the TF-IDF and LM similarities on the same pairs.

\textit{Partial correlation.} We computed the partial Spearman correlation of
each LM similarity matrix with each chromatin reference, controlling for
TF-IDF. For Atacformer (AC), this is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{LM,AC \mid TF} =
    \frac{\rho_{LM,AC} - \rho_{LM,TF}\,\rho_{AC,TF}}
         {\sqrt{(1-\rho_{LM,TF}^{2})(1-\rho_{AC,TF}^{2})}},
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{X,Y}$ is the Spearman correlation between the upper-triangle
entries of matrices $X$ and $Y$, estimated on $2 \times 10^6$ random pairs
(out of about 148 million pairs for well-covered experiments and 520 million
for the full dataset). A positive value means that the LM similarity is
associated with chromatin similarity beyond what TF-IDF explains. A value near
zero or below means that TF-IDF accounts for the LM's agreement with the
reference.

\textit{Cross top-$k$ overlap.} For each LM, we computed the mean fraction of
its top-$k$ nearest neighbors that are also top-$k$ TF-IDF neighbors
($k \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$ on the full dataset and $k = 5$ in well-covered
experiments). High overlap means that the LM's local structure largely
reproduces TF-IDF.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Peak Count Inflates Apparent Text-Chromatin Agreement}
\label{sec:coverage}

Experiments with few peaks consist mostly of promoter peaks
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}a). In every peak-count bin between 100 and 10,000
peaks, the median experiment had 69 to 75\% of its peaks within 1~kb of a TSS.
The fraction fell to 57\% at 10,000 to 20,000 peaks, 39\% at 20,000 to 50,000
peaks, and 24\% above 50,000 peaks. The 1,689 experiments with fewer than 100
peaks were an exception (median 42\%). Promoters of broadly expressed genes are
accessible in most cell types, so experiments that retain mainly promoter
peaks tend to resemble one another whatever their cell type.

Peak count is also strongly associated with similarity in both chromatin
representations. On the full dataset, Atacformer similarity correlated with
depth match (Section~\ref{sec:tech_methods}) at $\rho = 0.66$ and RP
similarity at $\rho = 0.59$, more strongly than any text embedding correlated
with either reference. After controlling for depth match and mean depth, the
experiment-level correlation between Atacformer and RP dropped from 0.22 to
$-0.02$. Peak count matters within cell types as well. Across 406 cell types
with at least 10 experiments, mean within-cell-type Atacformer similarity was
lower in cell types with a wider spread of peak counts (Spearman
$\rho = -0.77$ against the standard deviation of $\log_{10}$ peak count; RP:
$\rho = -0.71$).

Text-Atacformer agreement on the full dataset comes mostly from low-peak
experiments (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}b). When both experiments in a pair have
fewer than 1,000 peaks, text embeddings correlate with Atacformer at
$\rho = 0.38$ to $0.41$. The correlation drops to 0.08 to 0.09 at 1,000 to
5,000 peaks, 0.02 to 0.04 at 5,000 to 10,000 peaks, and 0.007 to 0.012 at
10,000 peaks or more. Agreement with RP behaves differently
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}c). It is lowest in the two low-peak strata
($\rho = 0.04$ to $0.10$) and higher in the two well-covered strata
($\rho = 0.08$ to $0.17$).

Within the metadata, most of the signal comes from sample titles. On the same
$2 \times 10^6$ pairs, a TF-IDF embedding of the title field alone correlated
with Atacformer as strongly as PubMedBERT applied to the full metadata string
($\rho = 0.275$ for both). Within cell types, where the other fields are
mostly constant, the title-only embedding exceeded every full-text model
($\rho = 0.24$ vs.\ 0.20 to 0.22), and controlling for title similarity
reduced the within-cell-type correlations of the text embeddings with
Atacformer to 0.04 to 0.06. The titles are archive sample names such as
``GSM3381886: F 2 p; Homo sapiens; ATAC-seq'' or ``Illumina HiSeq 2500
sequencing of SAMD00123421''. Many of them name only the submission or the
sequencing platform, so the shared signal most likely reflects data
provenance. Provenance is also tied to peak count. Controlling for depth
match and mean depth reduced the title-only correlation with Atacformer from
0.275 to 0.092. Cell-type labels, by comparison, explained little of the
full-dataset correlation. Controlling for shared cell type and shared
cell-type class reduced PubMedBERT's correlation with Atacformer only from
0.275 to 0.254.

We therefore base our primary analysis on well-covered experiments
($\geq$5,000 peaks). The threshold is pragmatic. Experiments with 5,000 to
10,000 peaks are still promoter-rich (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}a), but
experiment-level correlations computed with a 10,000-peak threshold lead to
the same conclusions.

\subsection{Text Weakly Tracks RP but Not Atacformer in Well-Covered Experiments}

As a sanity check, we embedded 34 representative cell types by averaging over
all of their experiments (Fig.~\ref{fig:s1}). Atacformer and RP showed
coherent lineage structure, with immune, neural, and cancer cell types forming
distinct groups, while the text embeddings showed coarser organization.

Table~\ref{tab:spearman} summarizes agreement in the 17,232 well-covered
experiments (954 cell types). Text embeddings showed no positive agreement
with Atacformer. At the cell-type level, the correlations of the full-text LMs
and TF-IDF with Atacformer were zero or slightly negative
($\rho = -0.074$ to $-0.023$; one-sided Mantel $p > 0.98$). At the experiment
level they were close to zero ($\rho = 0.011$ to $0.016$), compared with 0.20
to 0.30 on the full dataset. Agreement with RP was weak but consistent. The
full-text LMs and TF-IDF correlated with RP at $\rho = 0.038$ to $0.068$ at
the cell-type level (Mantel $p = 0.001$) and at 0.061 to 0.098 at the
experiment level. Atacformer and RP agreed with each other at the cell-type
level ($\rho = 0.365$) but only modestly at the experiment level
($\rho = 0.107$).

At the cell-type level, the name-only variants agreed with RP much less than
the full-text models ($\rho = 0.003$ to $0.021$ vs.\ 0.038 to 0.068), so the
description and title fields add information beyond the cell-type name. At
the experiment level the gap was smaller ($\rho = 0.035$ to $0.080$ for the
name-only variants).

\subsection{Language Models Add Little Beyond Vocabulary Overlap}
\label{sec:partial_corr}

In well-covered experiments, TF-IDF fell within the range of the four LMs on
every correlation in Table~\ref{tab:spearman}. After controlling for TF-IDF,
partial correlations with Atacformer were near zero for all four LMs
($\rho_{\mathrm{partial}} = 0.000$ to $0.006$; all bootstrap 95\% CIs within
$-0.004$ to $0.011$). Partial correlations with RP were small for MedCPT,
General, and SapBERT ($\rho_{\mathrm{partial}} = 0.046$, 0.046, and 0.037) and
near zero for PubMedBERT (0.003). LM and TF-IDF similarities were strongly
correlated ($\rho = 0.52$ to $0.73$), and 44 to 45\% of each LM's top-5
neighbors were also TF-IDF neighbors.

On the full dataset (Figs.~\ref{fig:decomp} to~\ref{fig:s3b}), PubMedBERT
($\rho_{\mathrm{partial}} = 0.051$; 95\% CI 0.048 to 0.059) and SapBERT
(0.045; CI 0.039 to 0.049) kept positive partial correlations with
Atacformer, whereas MedCPT ($-0.030$) and General ($-0.042$) did not. This
difference holds up under experiment-level resampling, but it disappears in
well-covered experiments and depends on corpus centering. Without centering,
PubMedBERT, SapBERT, and MedCPT all have clearly positive partial correlations
(0.14 to 0.26), and PubMedBERT ($\rho = 0.292$) has a higher raw correlation
with Atacformer than TF-IDF does ($\rho = 0.153$). We therefore do not read
the full-dataset differences among LMs as evidence about their pretraining or
fine-tuning objectives.

\subsection{Text Shares Local Structure with RP but Not Atacformer}

Nearest-neighbor overlap showed the same split between the two references.
In well-covered experiments, the full-text LMs shared 31 to 35\% of their
top-5 neighbors with RP (PubMedBERT 34.7\%, SapBERT 34.2\%, General 32.8\%,
MedCPT 30.9\%), TF-IDF shared 28.2\%, and the name-only variants about 12\%.
With Atacformer, every text embedding shared less than 0.1\% of its top-5
neighbors, against 0.03\% expected by chance, and Atacformer and RP shared
only 0.1\% with each other. On the full dataset the corresponding values were
20 to 23\% for RP and 2 to 3\% for Atacformer (Figs.~\ref{fig:topk}
to~\ref{fig:s2}).

Neighborhood purity helps explain the difference. In well-covered
experiments, 76\% of an experiment's top-5 RP neighbors shared its cell-type
label (93 to 96\% for the full-text embeddings), compared with 1.3\% for
Atacformer, barely above the 0.8\% expected by chance. On the full dataset the
values were 72\% for RP and 21\% for Atacformer. Within well-covered
experiments, Atacformer similarity was only weakly related to depth match
($\rho = 0.06$), but RP similarity was still strongly related to it
($\rho = 0.57$). Experiments of the same cell type were also far more similar
to each other under RP than under Atacformer (median within-cell-type
similarity 0.51 vs.\ 0.17 across 796 cell types with at least two
well-covered experiments; Atacformer lower for 96.7\% of cell types; Wilcoxon
signed-rank $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-125}$). The full-dataset versions of this
comparison are shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:s4} and~\ref{fig:s4b}.

RP thus reflects cell-type identity, which text metadata also encodes,
although RP remains sensitive to peak count. Individual Atacformer embeddings
of well-covered experiments, in contrast, carry little cell-type information.
Their nearest neighbors rarely share a cell type, even though cell-type
averages still agree with RP. The low agreement between text and Atacformer
is therefore not evidence that Atacformer captures finer biological variation
that text misses. One possible explanation, which we did not test, is that each
Atacformer embedding averages at most 1,024 randomly sampled peaks
(Section~\ref{sec:atacformer}), a small and variable fraction of the peaks in
a well-covered experiment.

Tables~\ref{tab:qual_atac} and~\ref{tab:qual_rp} list full-dataset pairs with
high chromatin similarity but low TF-IDF similarity. Many of these pairs
involve low-peak experiments. Every experiment in Table~\ref{tab:qual_rp} has
at most 1,438 peaks (the corpus median is 6,287), and the first pair in
Table~\ref{tab:qual_atac} (NCI-H1299 and CD4+ T cells) has 1,181 and 1,272
peaks. The remaining Atacformer pairs vary more in peak count.

% ── Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

Our results put quantitative bounds on how well experimental metadata can
stand in for ATAC-seq chromatin similarity, and they show that these bounds
depend on data quality. On the full ChIP-Atlas set, text embeddings correlated
with Atacformer similarity at $\rho = 0.20$ to $0.30$. Most of this agreement
came from low-peak experiments, whose promoter-dominated peak sets make them
look alike in both chromatin representations, and from sample titles, which
reflect data provenance and are tied to peak count. Among well-covered
experiments, text embeddings barely tracked Atacformer similarity.

The agreement that remained was between text and RP ($\rho = 0.06$ to $0.10$
at the experiment level, with 28 to 35\% of top-5 neighbors shared). RP
neighborhoods are organized largely by cell type, and text metadata recovers
this coarse structure. Metadata may therefore be useful for coarse,
cell-type-level retrieval, but it should not substitute for chromatin
similarity in finer-grained applications.

Language models added little. In well-covered experiments TF-IDF matched
every LM, and partial correlations beyond TF-IDF were near zero for Atacformer
and small for RP. The apparent advantage of PubMedBERT and SapBERT over MedCPT
and General on the full dataset did not survive restriction to well-covered
experiments, and it changed with corpus centering.

The peak-count effect matters beyond text. In both chromatin
representations, similarity was more strongly associated with peak count than
with any other variable we examined, and controlling for peak count removed
the experiment-level agreement between Atacformer and RP on the full dataset.
Studies that compare chromatin embeddings across heterogeneous public
repositories should stratify by peak count or control for it, along with
other quality measures.

This study has several limitations. Atacformer and RP serve as proxies for
chromatin biology without validation against an external ground truth. Both
are derived from the same ChIP-Atlas peak calls, both are sensitive to peak
count, and the Atacformer embeddings used here carry little cell-type
information for individual well-covered experiments. Our conclusions are
therefore bounded by how faithfully these representations capture the
biology they stand for. The metadata we used did not include sequencing depth
or standard quality metrics such as the fraction of reads in peaks or TSS
enrichment, so we relied on peak count as a quality proxy, and the 5,000-peak
threshold is a pragmatic choice. We also limited the text to metadata fields
available in ChIP-Atlas and did not test multimodal embeddings that combine
text and chromatin signals. Future work could re-embed experiments using all
of their peaks, use uniformly reprocessed and quality-controlled data, and
test whether text encoders trained to predict chromatin similarity can narrow
the gap.
